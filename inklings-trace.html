Trace Bench — the nib engine & the font scorer

M1 + M2 + M2.5 + M7 of the Scriptorium (docs/inklings-typography.md). Trace the letter stroke by stroke; the ink is a nib stamped along your path — §3's claim that stroke contrast is a record of the tool. Glyphs come from Wordshape's authored alphabet (data/wordshape-alphabet.json if present, else sliced live out of wordshape-draw.html so the two can't drift).

M2.5 moves the ground under the grade (§5.7). You are no longer scored against the skeleton — you are scored against the real typeface, so your a in Blackletter has to look like a blackletter a. The skeleton drops back to being the guide and the stroke order, the one thing a font file can't supply (fonts ship outlines, not strokes); the face's own glyph is ghosted under the pad; and the grade is a chamfer distance field over your stamped ink: coverage (how much of the letter you filled) and fidelity (how much of your ink landed on it), combined as F-beta with coverage weighted 2:1 (which is beta = √2 — see the Tuning panel). The payoff is that the nib stops being a lookup and becomes the only way to pass — too thin and you can't fill the letter, too fat and your ink spills off it, wrong angle and both fail. There is no verdict table, no smudge and no angle tolerance any more.

M2 adds the faces. data/nibs.json and data/typefaces.json now hold the pens and the pruned Vox-ATypI tree, and fonts/ holds one OFL face per classification leaf — so the thing you are scored against is a genuine typeface, not our approximation. Pick a face, pick the pen you think wrote it, and the cells below the pad are the argument side by side: the target, the skeleton in that face's own nib, your ink drawn on the target, and the album cell.

M7 adds the capitals (§10) — a second alphabet, not more letters. Roman inscriptional capitals were laid out with a flat brush and cut with a chisel eight centuries before the Carolingian pen wrote the minuscules, which is why a capital is not a big lowercase: it stops at cap height, below the ascender, and its widths are a system (O a circle, E narrow, M wide). Switch with A–Z or shift+key. Each face keeps two fractions, not one of 52 — and Uncial has no case at all, so its A really is its a.

· a–z picks a letter, shift+key its capital · 1–9 picks a nib

Press and drag to write one stroke at a time, in the order a hand would, following the ghosted letter — that ghost is literally the mask you're scored against. The numbered dot is where the next stroke starts. A stroke that runs backwards is refused and shows you the ghost (§5.2's gate, and the handwriting lesson); starting off the dot is now only a note, because the skeleton's start is the wrong place to stand for a face whose letterform genuinely differs.

    Tuning

    Off-the-letter falloff and coverage tolerance are the two to argue about — between them they decide whether a single stamped nib can fill a Didone's hairlines or a blackletter's sharp corners at all, which is the one way §5.7 could fail. F-beta weights coverage by beta squared, so the default 1.41 is the 2:1 the doc asks for. Push it to 2 (i.e. 4:1) and you can scribble over the ghost and earn the face — measured, against the 0.72 threshold: clean trace 0.90 / too-fat pen 0.78 / scribble 0.77. At 1.41 those read 0.90 / 0.69 / 0.66, so only the trace passes. Pull it to 1 and coverage stops outweighing fidelity at all, and a small cautious mark starts to look attractive. §5.3's snap is gone — it pulled strokes toward the skeleton, which is no longer the target, so snapping now would drag your ink off the letter. A scruffy stroke stays scruffy (doc §13 #8).
